1    1       Purpose.
2            This advisory circular (AC) provides standards for the planning, design, and
3            construction of takeoff and landing facilities serving vertical flight aircraft.


4    2       Cancellation.
5            This AC cancels AC 150/5390-2D, Heliport Design, dated January 5, 2023, and
6            Engineering Brief 105A, Vertiport Design, dated December 27, 2024.


7    3       Applicability.
 8           This AC establishes Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) standards and guidelines
 9           for Vertical Flight Aircraft Infrastructure Design. This AC does not constitute a
10           regulation and is not legally binding in its own right. It will not be relied upon as a
11           separate basis by the FAA for affirmative enforcement action or other administrative
12           penalty. Conformity with this AC is voluntary, and nonconformity will not affect rights
13           and obligations under existing statutes and regulations, except as described below.
14           Where applicable, the standards contained in this AC provide an acceptable means of
15           satisfying the requirements of Title14 Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) Part 139,
16           Certification of Airports; and the requirements for federally obligated airport operators
17           per Grant Assurance 19 and other applicable Grant Assurances. For all other airport
18           operators, conformity with this AC is recommended. The FAA has determined the
19           standards and guidelines contained in this AC are practices which provide an acceptable
20           level of safety, performance and in support of operations for Vertical Flight Aircraft
21           Infrastructure Design.

200                                      CHAPTER 1. Introduction


201   1.1     Background.
202           The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) developed standards for heliport design
203           starting in 1959, updating them as research and development was produced throughout
204           the years that warranted changes. Advisory Circular (AC) 150/5390-2, Heliport Design,
205           historically provided standards for the planning, design, and construction of heliports
206           serving helicopters with single, tandem (front and rear), or dual (side by side) rotors.
207           The FAA previously provided guidance for vertiports in AC 150/5390-3, Vertiport
208           Design, published on May 31, 1991. The AC was based on civil tiltrotors modeled after
209           military tiltrotor technology and was cancelled on July 28, 2010. With the introduction
210           of new electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft, the FAA identified a need
211           to provide guidance for facilities serving these new aircraft. As a result, Engineering
212           Brief (EB) 105, Vertiport Design, was published in 2022. Due to similarities between
213           heliports and vertiports, EB 105 was updated to EB 105A, Vertiport Design, as a
214           supplement to AC 150/5390-2.
215           This AC evolves heliport design to include standards for heliports serving all vertical
216           flight aircraft. Heliports with design elements distinct for powered lift aircraft are also
217           commonly known as “vertiports.” Throughout the document, the term “heliport” refers
218           to all facilities designed for vertical flight aircraft, including vertiports, unless
219           specifically stated otherwise. Additionally, terms such as “helistop” are used by
220           industry and are considered heliports. Medical emergency sites used to provide
221           emergency medical transport at an accident scene are not considered heliports as
222           defined in this AC.


223   1.2     Standards, Recommended Practices, and Requirements.
224           The promulgation of the standards and recommendations contained in this AC advances
225           the goals and objectives of the FAA’s statutory authorities and the national policies
226           established by Congress.
227           The standards and recommendations in this AC do not limit or regulate the operations
228           of aircraft or heliports. When it is not feasible to meet the standards and
229           recommendations in this AC, or new aircraft entrants not addressed herein have an
230           interest in operating at heliports, consult with the appropriate offices in the FAA’s
231           Office of Airports and Flight Standards Service to identify possible modifications to
232           these standards and/or operational procedures that will accommodate safe heliport
233           standards.

234   1.2.1   Meaning of Terms.

235           1.2.1.1       Standard.
236                         A physical characteristic, quality, configuration, function, operation, or
237                         procedure established by the FAA as a benchmark for uniformity, safety,
238                         capacity, performance, economy, and environmental quality.

252   1.3     Definitions.
253           The Pilot/Controller Glossary of the Aeronautical Information Manual (AIM) defines
254           terms used in the Air Traffic System. The AIM is available from the FAA website
255           https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/. Other terms used in this publication
256           follow.
257           1. Aiming Point. Aiming points are marked locations that serve as a specific position
258              and visual cue for vertical flight aircraft to approach. An aiming point is not a
259              helipad.
260           2. Approach/Departure Path. The flight track an aircraft follows when landing at or
261              departing from a heliport. The approach/departure paths may be straight or curved.
262           3. Battery. One or more electrically connected cells, assembled in a single container
263              having positive and negative terminals. A battery may include inter-cell connectors
264              and other devices.
265           4. Battery Pack. Two or more battery systems.
266           5. Battery System. Comprised of the battery, the battery charger, and any protective,
267              monitoring, and alerting circuitry or hardware inside or outside of the battery. It also
268              includes vents (where necessary) and packaging.
269           6. Controlling Dimension (D). The diameter of the smallest circle enclosing the entire
270              aircraft projection on a horizontal plane, including all possible configurations with
271              rotors/propellers turning, if applicable (Figure 1-1).

274                     7. Critical Aircraft. The most demanding aircraft type, or grouping of aircraft, with
275                        similar physical and operational characteristics, expected to operate at the heliport.
276                        The critical aircraft can be a composite of several different aircraft and composed of
277                        the most demanding characteristics of each that is expected to use the facility (e.g.,
278                        the maximum weight, maximum contact load/minimum contact area, controlling
279                        dimension (D), overall aircraft width (OW), rotor diameter (RD), tail rotor arc
280                        radius, undercarriage dimensions, and pilot’s eye height of all aircraft). If a heliport
281                        sponsor is using FAA grant funds in the facility, the critical aircraft must meet the
282                        requirements specified in AC 150/5000-17, Critical Aircraft and Regular Use
283                        Determination.
284                     8. Design Loads.
285                        a. Static Load. For design purposes, the design static load is equal to the critical
286                           aircraft’s maximum takeoff weight applied through the total contact area of the
287                           wheels or skids. Loads are applied through the contact area of the tires for
288                           wheel-equipped aircraft or the contact area of the skid for skid-equipped aircraft.
289                           Consult with the aircraft’s manufacturer to obtain the contact area for the
290                           specific aircraft of interest.
291                        b. Dynamic Load. A dynamic load of 0.2 seconds or less duration may occur
292                           during a hard landing. For design purposes, assume dynamic loads at 150
293                           percent of the maximum takeoff weight of the critical aircraft. When specific
294                           loading data is not available, assume 75 percent of the weight of the critical
295                           aircraft is applied equally through the contact area of the rear two wheels (or the
296                           pair of rear wheels of a dual-wheel configuration) of a wheel-equipped aircraft.
297                           For a skid-equipped aircraft, assume 75 percent of the weight of the critical
298                           aircraft is applied equally through the aft contact areas of the two skids of a
299                           skid-equipped aircraft (Figure 4-2). Consult aircraft manufacturers to obtain the
300                           aft contact area for specific aircraft of interest.

301           9. Downwash/Outwash (DWOW). The downward and outward movement of air caused
302              by the action of rotating rotor blade, propeller, or ducted fan. When this air strikes
303              the ground or some other surface, it causes a turbulent outflow of air from the
304              aircraft.
305           10. Downwash/Outwash Caution Area (DCA). An operational area established where
306               downwash/outwash (DWOW) potentially meets or exceeds 34.5 mph.
307           11. Elevated Heliport. A heliport located on a rooftop or other elevated structure where
308               the touchdown and liftoff area (TLOF) is at least 30 inches (0.8 m) above the
309               surrounding surface (a ground-level heliport with the TLOF on a mound made of
310               earth is not an elevated heliport).
311           12. Effective Translational Lift (ETL). A phenomenon that occurs when the rotor/lifting
312               blade system becomes more efficient with forward air speed as the rotor/lifting
313               system disk outruns the recirculation of old vortices into undisturbed air resulting in
314               less induced flow and drag on the rotor/lifting system.
315           13. Emergency Helicopter Landing Facility (EHLF). A clear area at ground level or on
316               the roof of a building capable of accommodating aircraft engaged in firefighting
317               and/or emergency evacuation operations and are not for routine aircraft operations.
318               An EHLF meets the definition of a heliport in this AC and under 14 CFR Part 157,
319               Notice of Construction, Alteration, Activation, and Deactivation of Airports.
320           14. Final Approach and Takeoff Area (FATO). A defined area over which the pilot
321               completes the final phase of the approach to a hover or a landing and from which
322               the pilot initiates takeoff. The FATO and TLOF are normally co-located but may be
323               located separately. The FATO is associated with all instrument approach/departure
324               procedures. See paragraph 4.3.
325           15. Frangible Mounting. A physical characteristic whereby an object retains its
326               structural integrity and stiffness up to a designated maximum load, but upon impact
327               from a greater load, breaks, distorts, or yields in such a manner as to present the
328               minimum hazard to aircraft.
329           16. Ground Effect. A condition of usually improved aerodynamic performance
330               encountered when the aircraft is operating very close to the ground or a surface. It
331               results from a reduction in upwash, downwash, and/or blade tip vortices, which
332               provide a corresponding decrease in induced drag.
333           17. Hazard to Air Navigation. An existing or proposed object that the FAA, as a result
334               of an aeronautical study, determines will have a substantial adverse effect upon the
335               safe and efficient use of navigable airspace by aircraft, operation of air navigation
336               facilities, or existing or potential airport capacity.
337           18. Helipad. A small, designated area, usually with a prepared surface, on a heliport or
338               an airport, used for takeoff, landing, or parking of vertical flight aircraft. A helipad
339               is a TLOF and its associated FATO, and safety area, and approach/departure
340               surfaces. The sizing and dimension criteria are based on the critical aircraft. A
341               vertipad is a helipad designed to the specific requirements to serve powered lift
342               aircraft.

343                19. Heliport. An area of land, water, or structure used or intended to be used for
344                    landings and takeoffs by vertical flight aircraft and includes associated buildings
345                    and facilities. Two distinct types of heliports that warrant further definition include
346                    the following:
347                    a. Hospital. A hospital heliport services aircraft used by air ambulance providers.
348                       A hospital heliport may be designed to accommodate large military aircraft in
349                       some emergencies. Air ambulance aircraft are often used to transport injured
350                       people from the scene of an accident to a hospital and to transfer patients from
351                       one hospital to another. A designated landing area located at a hospital or
352                       medical facility is a heliport and not a medical emergency site.
353                    b. Vertiport. A heliport that is designed to the specific requirements to serve
354                       powered lift aircraft. 1
355                20. Heliport Elevation. The highest elevation of all aircraft landing areas (TLOFs)
356                    within the heliport, expressed as the distance above mean sea level (MSL).
357                21. Heliport Imaginary Surfaces. The imaginary planes defined in 14 CFR Part 77,
358                    Safe, Efficient Use, and Preservation of the Navigable Airspace, centered about the
359                    FATO and the approach/departure paths, which are used to identify the objects
360                    where notice to and evaluation by the FAA is required. Imaginary surfaces include
361                    the primary surface, approach/departure surfaces, and transitional surfaces.
362                22. Heliport Layout Plan. The plan of a heliport showing the layout of existing and
363                    proposed heliport facilities including all safety related dimensional standards
364                    (approach/departure paths and dimensions of TLOF, FATO, safety area, heliport
365                    protections zone (HPZ), and all other standards described in this AC). See AC
366                    150/5300-20, Submission of On-Airport Proposals for Aeronautical Study,
367                    paragraph 1.9.5 (defining “Airport” to include heliports).

533                             CHAPTER 2. Roles and Responsibilities


534   2.1     Notification Requirements.
535           14 CFR Part 157 sets requirements for persons proposing to construct, activate,
536           deactivate, or alter a heliport to give advance notice of their intent to the FAA. Failure
537           to give notice is subject to civil penalty under Title 49 U.S.C. 46301, Civil Penalties.
538           This includes but is not limited to the following changes:
539           1. changing the size or number of FATOs;
540           2. adding, deleting, or changing an approach or departure route;
541           3. change a heliport status (for example, private to public use or vice versa).
542           For development on non-federally obligated airports or heliports or for non-federally
543           funded standalone heliport sites, and in compliance with 14 CFR Part 157 the proponent
544           must submit FAA Form 7480-1, Notice for Construction, Alteration and Deactivation
545           of Airports, at least 90 days in advance of the day that construction work is to begin on
546           the takeoff and landing facility (see § 157.5 for other scenarios). Note: The 90-day
547           advance notice requirement governs when the proponent must submit Form 7480-1; it
548           does not establish a timeline for the FAA’s airspace determination, which proceeds
549           independently. The FAA encourages proponents to engage the appropriate FAA
550           Airports Regional or District Office before submitting Form 7480-1; however, the
551           FAA’s formal evaluation cannot begin until a complete Form 7480-1 is received.
552           For non-federally obligated heliport facilities that are modified in geometry to a
553           different type of heliport, the sponsor must submit a new Form 7480-1 at least 90 days
554           in advance of the day that work is to begin. See 14 CFR Part 157. The Form 7480-1 can
555           be submitted electronically as a Digital 7480 at https://adip.faa.gov. The FAA’s Flight
556           Standards organization will determine when to do an onsite evaluation using risk-based
557           analysis (FAA Order 8900.1, Flight Standards Information Management System).
558           For vertical flight infrastructure development on airports or heliports identified under
559           14 CFR § 77.9(d), a sponsor must submit a Form 7460-1, Notice of Proposed
560           Construction or Alteration, at least 45 days before the start date of the proposed
561           construction or alteration or the date an application for a construction permit is filed,
562           whichever is earliest. Guidance is in AC 150/5300-20.
563           For federally obligated airports, vertical flight infrastructure or equipment must be
564           depicted on the ALP. See AC 150/5070-6, Airport Master Plans. Construction must not
565           begin until the FAA has completed its review of the ALP and airspace case and issued a
566           non-objectionable airspace determination.
567           Do not begin construction on public use unobligated airports or heliports until a non-
568           objectionable airspace determination has been issued.
569           Coordinate potential sites with any nearby landing facilities and state and local officials.
570           Lack of early coordination can cause airspace, operational, safety, capacity, and
571           financial impacts. While the FAA will review all new heliport proposals for the safe and
572           efficient utilization of navigable airspace by aircraft and the safety of people and


                                                     2-1
      10/22/2026                                  DRAFT                                   AC 150/5390-4


573           property on the ground, early coordination with all applicable entities offers insight into
574           airspace and capacity conflicts before investments are made.

656                                 CHAPTER 3. Infrastructure Siting


657   3.1     Site Selection.
658           Proper heliport siting is critical to ensuring safe, efficient, and compatible facility
659           operations for helicopters and powered lift aircraft. Proper site selection is comprised of
660           numerous components, including compatible land use planning, utility coordination,
661           analysis of wind and turbulence created by objects, and approach/departure path
662           selection. The changing dynamics of the built environment around heliports can
663           negatively impact the heliport’s operations, requiring a continued effort to ensure the
664           site selected remains viable for heliport operations and the protection of the heliport
665           investment. As novel aircraft designs are incorporated into heliports, careful site
666           selection planning is vital to the long-term success of a facility.

667   3.1.1   Long-Term Planning.
668           The future facility needs and surrounding land uses are critical to long-term planning
669           and ensuring clear approach/departure surfaces to maintain the utility of the site.

670           3.1.1.1      Recommended Practices.
671                        1. Consider the possible future need for instrument operations, alternative
672                           fuels (including electrification), and facility expansion when planning
673                           a heliport.
674                        2. Consider any current or future potential use by powered lift, larger,
675                           and/or military aircraft during planning.
676                        3. Plan for critical aircraft with distinct approach/departure surfaces that
677                           may differ from 8:1 slope.
678                        4. Evaluate local electrical grid capacity and utility constraints.

679   3.1.2   Property Size.
680           The property needed for a heliport depends on the volume and types of users, aircraft
681           types and sizes, and the scope of amenities provided for each type of heliport facility.
682           Property for aircraft operators and for passenger amenities may also be needed. The
683           area needed for heliport operations minimally includes a cleared area absent of
684           structures that will create turbulence for the aircraft, a wind indicator, a clear
685           approach/departure path, and a DCA.

686           3.1.2.1      Recommended Practice.
687                        Consider the property, land use, and design of areas adjacent to the
688                        heliport for HPZ or DCAs.

689           3.1.2.2      Design Consideration.
690                        Mitigation efforts may limit the DWOW effects that can be felt beyond the
691                        property boundary.



                                                     3-1
      10/22/2026                                  DRAFT                                    AC 150/5390-4


692   3.1.3   Turbulence.
693           Air flowing around and over buildings, stands of trees, terrain irregularities, etc., can
694           create turbulence on ground-level and roof-top heliports that may affect aircraft
695           operations.

696           3.1.3.1       Standards.
697                         1. Assess the turbulence and airflow characteristics near and across the
698                            surface of the TLOF and associated FATO, and along the final section
699                            of the approach/departure path to determine if an air gap among the
700                            roof, roof parapet or supporting structure, and/or some other
701                            turbulence mitigating design measure is necessary for elevated
702                            heliports.
703                         2. Perform the assessment where the TLOF and associated FATO is
704                            located near the edge and top of a building or structure, or within the
705                            influence of turbulent wakes from other buildings or structures (refer
706                            to the FAA’s Technical Report FAA/RD-84/25).
707                         3. Locate the landing and takeoff area away from buildings, trees, and
708                            other large objects to minimize air turbulence near the TLOF, FATO,
709                            safety area, and associated approach/departure paths.
710                         4. For rooftop heliports:
711                             a. Locate the TLOF, FATO, and safety area at or above the highest
712                                point of the main structure and in positions that carry the
713                                prevailing winds away from the approach path.
714                             b. Assess the air flow around the heliport using airflow and
715                                turbulence measuring equipment.
716                             c. Establishing a 6-foot (1.8 m) or more air gap on all sides above the
717                                roof level will generally minimize the turbulent effect of air
718                                flowing over the roof edge.
719                                 i.     If an air gap is included in the elevated heliport design,
720                                        keep it free of objects that would obstruct the airflow.
721                                 ii.    Where it is impractical to include an air gap or other
722                                        turbulence mitigating design measures, operational
723                                        limitations may be necessary under certain wind conditions.


724   3.2     Compatible Land Use and Zoning.
725           Controlling the use of property around the heliport, including the HPZ and the
726           approach/departure path environment, and restricting activities around the heliport to
727           those that are compatible with aircraft operations will ensure the long-term viability of
728           the heliport.




                                                      3-2
      10/22/2026                                 DRAFT                                  AC 150/5390-4


729   3.2.1   Recommended Practices.
730           1. Promote the adoption of state and local measures such as zoning and/or the purchase
731              of air rights and property easements.
732           2. Review general guidance on compatible land uses in AC 150/5190-4, Airport Land
733              Use Compatibility Planning. While this is tailored to airports, many of the same
734              issues will apply to heliports.
735           3. Plan for noise compatibility around the heliport.


736   3.3     Selection of Approach/Departure Paths.
737           Wind, obstructions, and environmental considerations impact the number and location
738           of approach/departure paths. Create multiple approach/departure paths to increase
739           flexibility and utility.

740   3.3.1   Wind.

741           3.3.1.1      Standards.
742                        1. Use a wind analysis to determine the primary approach path to the
743                           heliport (AC 150/5300-13B, Airport Design, Appendix B).
744                            a. The FAA recommends a data period covering at least the last ten
745                               consecutive years of wind observations for this analysis.
746                            b. Include calm wind conditions in the wind data.
747                        2. Align the preferred approach/departure paths with the predominant
748                           wind direction as much as possible to avoid downwind operations and
749                           keep crosswind operations to a minimum.
750                        3. Provide at least two approach/departure paths that are reciprocal in
751                           magnetic heading or, when not feasible, are at least 135 degrees apart.
752                        4. Ensure the primary approach/departure path alignment supports the
753                           performance envelope of the critical aircraft, recognizing that some
754                           aircraft may have limited crosswind capability and may require
755                           additional wind direction indicator placement.

756           3.3.1.2      Design Consideration.
757                        If it is not feasible to provide complete coverage of wind conditions
758                        through multiple approach/departure paths, operational limitations may be
759                        necessary under certain wind conditions.

760   3.3.2   Obstructions.
761           Once established, 14 CFR Part 77 provides the standards for identifying obstructions to
762           air navigation, and the FAA utilizes three such surfaces defined to evaluate and protect
763           a public use heliport and a heliport with at least one FAA-approved instrument
764           approach procedure: the primary surface, the approach and departure surface, and the


                                                    3-3
      10/22/2026                                  DRAFT                                    AC 150/5390-4


765           transitional surface. The FAA presumes obstructions to these 14 CFR Part 77 surfaces
766           are hazards to air navigation unless further aeronautical study concludes otherwise.

767           3.3.2.1       Standard.
768                         Mitigate obstructions to air navigation by realigning approach/departure
769                         paths or removal, lowering, marking, and lighting of obstructions.

770           3.3.2.2       Design Consideration.
771                         Consider any proposed (future) obstructions near the heliport and those
772                         likely to be a hazard to air navigation.

773   3.3.3   Environmental Considerations.
774           Typical environmental considerations include wildlife, noise, electric tonal emissions,
775           electromagnetic compatibility (EMC), and battery hazardous energy release impacts.

776           3.3.3.1       Standard.
777                         Select the final approach/departure path(s) to minimize any environmental
778                         impact, providing it does not decrease flight safety.


779   3.4     Vertical Flight Infrastructure on Airports.
780           Operations can occur on existing airport infrastructure for its intended purpose or on
781           dedicated infrastructure (helipad or aiming point). Airports may have operational or
782           capacity constraints on their runways that require dedicated vertical flight infrastructure.

784           When dedicated vertical flight infrastructure is determined necessary, it can be in the
785           form of a helipad or vertical flight aiming point.

786           3.4.1.1       Standards.
787                         1. Site this infrastructure in locations that do not interfere with airplane
788                            traffic and operations or impact capacity.
789                            a. Site a helipad when required by the FAA for safety, operational,
790                               airspace, or infrastructure considerations.
791                            b. Site a helipad when developing instrument flight rules (IFR)
792                               procedures to the helipad.
793                         2. Ensure helipad lighting does not conflict with airport lighting.
794                         3. Follow Chapter 6 for designing vertical flight aircraft parking
795                            positions on airports.

796           3.4.1.2       Recommended Practice.
797                         Site vertical flight aiming points at facilities having no more than ten VFR
798                         piloted vertical flight operations per day.

851   3.5     Air Traffic Considerations for Vertical Flight Infrastructure Siting.
852           When selecting a site, understand how the following air traffic considerations may
853           impact the proposed site operations. Failing to consider these in the site selection
854           process may result in delays or operational limitations on the proposed site, reducing its
855           operational efficiency. FAA Order JO 7110.65, Air Traffic Control, establishes
856           applicable operating criteria which are applied to locate the site.

857   3.5.1   Impacts Due to Wake Turbulence Separation.
858           To avoid dangerous wake turbulence encounters, apply appropriate wake turbulence
859           lateral and vertical separation between an airborne vertical flight aircraft and larger
860           aircraft.

861           3.5.1.1      Design Considerations.
862                        1. The closer a helipad FATO is to a runway, the more likely wake
863                           turbulence will impact its operations. As such:
864                            a. A FATO located less than 2,500 feet (762 m) from the runway
865                               centerline may impact both runway and helipad operations. At this
866                               distance, the effects of wake turbulence must be mitigated for
867                               airborne vertical flight aircraft during simultaneous operations with
868                               most aircraft.
869                            b. A FATO located 2,500 feet (762 m) or more from a runway
870                               centerline has the greatest potential for independent flight paths
871                               and minimal disruption to runway operations.
872                        2. Vertical flight aircraft operating under VFR must be able to enter
873                           ingress/egress routes at an altitude at least 500 feet (152 m)
874                           above/below aircraft arriving or departing from the runway.
875                        3. If the vertical flight aircraft is following or passing underneath a
876                           heavy/super aircraft, a minimum of 1,000 feet (305 m) separation is
877                           required.

878   3.5.2   Impacts Due to Missed Approach Procedures for Fixed-Wing Aircraft.
879           Many airports have instrument approach procedures to safely guide aircraft to land
880           when cloud ceilings or visibility are reduced. One important component of an
881           instrument approach procedure is the missed approach procedure. A missed approach
882           procedure gives pilots instructions on how to proceed in case a safe landing cannot be
883           continued. When an aircraft is on final approach, ATC ensures that the missed approach

                                                     3-7
      10/22/2026                                   DRAFT                                    AC 150/5390-4


884           procedure path is clear of other traffic. At airports with a significant number of
885           operations, this can cause additional delays to vertical flight aircraft arriving or
886           departing the helipad.

887           3.5.2.1       Standard.
888                         Coordinate with the affected air traffic facility to identify and account for
889                         any published and non-published local missed approach procedures.

890   3.5.3   Impacts Due to Departure Procedures.
891           Existing Area Navigation (RNAV) instrument departures (or other departure
892           procedures) allowing aircraft to fly headings that diverge from the runway centerline
893           may limit arrival/departure flexibility from the helipad and must be assessed on a site-
894           specific basis.

895   3.5.4   Impacts Due to Visual Flight Pattern.
896           Airports have established visual traffic patterns to better organize the flow of VFR
897           aircraft arrivals and departures. These traffic patterns are typically 1,000 feet (305 m)
898           AGL and can be on either side of a runway.
899           At airports with a significant number of VFR operations, the visual traffic pattern can
900           become congested, with numerous aircraft in the pattern simultaneously. For this
901           reason, a helipad that is located below the visual traffic pattern may experience
902           additional delays as controllers sequence vertical flight aircraft arrivals and departures
903           with aircraft in the visual traffic pattern.

904   3.5.5   Heliport Location Off the End of Airport Runways.
905           Heliports located near the approach or departure end of an airport runway may present
906           significant challenges for ATC.

907           3.5.5.1       Design Consideration.
908                         Locations closer to the runway arrival threshold or departure ends will
909                         present greater difficulty than those farther away and incur more delays
910                         and/or operational restrictions.

912           This chapter provides guidance on the design of heliports and helipads based on the
913           critical aircraft chosen.


914   4.1     Design Approach.
915           A heliport consists of the following:
916           1. A TLOF that is typically located within a FATO but can be located separate from
917              the FATO. See paragraph 4.2.
918           2. A FATO where arriving aircraft terminate their approach and from which departing
919              aircraft take off. See paragraph 4.3.
920           3. A safety area which surrounds the TLOF and FATO. See paragraph 4.4.
921           4. An HPZ, which is recommended for all heliports and required for those with IFR
922              procedures. See paragraph 4.5.
923           5. A DCA, which is an operational area in effect when aircraft are operating. See
924              paragraph 4.6.
925           6. Approach/departure surfaces to allow safe approaches to and departures from the
926              FATO. See paragraph 4.7.
927           The design standards in this chapter assume that there will never be more than one
928           aircraft within the FATO and its associated safety area. See Figure 4-1 and Figure A-1.

937   4.2.1   Standards.
938           1. Every heliport has at least one TLOF. The TLOF can be at ground level or elevated
939              (e.g., on top of a building).
940           2. A TLOF is considered elevated if it is located on a building top or other elevated
941              structure where the TLOF and associated FATO are at least 30 inches (0.8 m) above
942              the surrounding surface.

943           4.2.1.1      Location.
944                        1. Locate the TLOF on the ground, elevated structures, water-based
945                           vessels, or water-based stationary structures.
946                        2. Every TLOF is associated with at least one FATO, with the TLOF
947                           either located within a FATO or separate from it.
948                        3. When located within the FATO, the TLOF is centered within the
949                           FATO and along the major axis of the FATO.
950                        4. When located separately from the FATO, the TLOF is centered within
951                           its own safety area.

953                           water-based facilities only if there are sufficient unobstructed area and
954                           airspace for the aircraft to air/hover taxi from the FATO to the TLOF.

956                           air intakes or pedestrian areas.

1205   4.6       Downwash/Outwash Caution Area (DCA).
1206             Downwash is the airflow created by the propulsion units producing lift in vertical flight.
1207             When the downwash hits the ground, it moves horizontally as outwash. The DWOW
1208             created from the propulsion units of aircraft can be dangerous to people, property,
1209             equipment, and other aircraft. This downward and outward flow can result in ground
1210             surface erosion, wake recirculation, foreign object debris (FOD) and wind hazards. Air
1211             velocities of approximately 34.5 mph (55.5 kph) or greater can impact heliport safety 5.

1212   4.6.1     Standards.
1213             1. Establish DCAs anywhere that DWOW wind velocity can potentially meet or
1214                exceed 34.5 mph (55.5 kph).
1215             2. Design DCAs in the form of operational boundaries, or areas of restriction/control
1216                on movement of people during aircraft operations.
1217             3. The DCA is in effect during any aircraft operations creating DWOW.
1218             4. Design the DCA size to accommodate all aircraft types operating at the facility that
1219                will generate the strongest DWOW effect.
1220                 a. Use the greatest anticipated DWOW effect to determine distances used in the
1221                    design considerations, signage, or movement restrictions.
1222             5. For aircraft 7,000 lbs and lighter, design the DCA to be a minimum of 420 feet (128
1223                m) wide and 780 feet (238 m) long centered on the TLOF/FATO under each
1224                approach and departure surface (Figure 4-8).
1225             6. For aircraft 7,000 lbs and lighter, attach a 210-foot (64 m) radius DCA centered on
1226                the aircraft to wherever the aircraft will taxi (Figure 4-8).

         Rotorwash Analysis Handbook, Federal Aviation Administration, 1994, p. 317, “…the majority of rotorwash-
       related mishaps can be avoided if separation distances are maintained so that impacting rotorwash-generated
       velocities do not exceed 30 to 40 knots across the ground.”

1259   4.7     VFR Approach/Departure Surfaces and Obstruction Identification.
1260           The approach, departure, and transitional surfaces provide clear airspace to allow safe
1261           approach and departure to and from the FATO. A heliport approach/departure surface
1262           (herein referred to as “approach surface”) may be different from approach preservation
1263           surfaces found in the 14 CFR Part 77 and used by the FAA for obstruction identification
1264           (see paragraph 4.7.2). The approach slope flown by vertical flight aircraft may be
1265           shallower than this slope, especially if the aircraft needs HIGE because of takeoff
1266           weight, outside air temperature, or altitude or the aircraft has been certified in the
1267           powered lift category. While the maximum approach surface for a heliport is 8:1 and
1268           this is the only slope the FAA considers when evaluating obstructions under 14 CFR
1269           Part 77, a heliport sponsor may choose to design a shallower approach slope.
1270           VFR approach and departure surfaces may be curved; however, only the straight VFR
1271           approach/departure and transitional surfaces are addressed in 14 CFR Part 77, Safe,
1272           Efficient Use and Preservation of the Navigable Airspace. Therefore, while they may be
1273           used, curved approaches are not evaluated by the FAA for the effect of objects
1274           (temporary or permanent, existing or new) on aeronautical operating procedures. These
1275           curved approaches are also not considered in aeronautical charting for new objects.

1355   4.7.2   14 CFR Part 77 Imaginary Surfaces for Obstruction Identification.
1356           The imaginary surfaces defined in 14 CFR Part 77, for heliports include the primary
1357           surface, approach, and transitional surfaces. 14 CFR Part 77 establishes standards and
1358           notification requirements for objects affecting navigable airspace. This notification
1359           provides the basis for:
1360           •    evaluating the effect of construction or alteration on aeronautical operating
1361                procedures.
1362           •    determining the potential hazardous effect of proposed construction on air
1363                navigation.
1364           •    identifying mitigating measures to enhance safe air navigation; and
1365           •    aeronautical charting for new objects.
1366           The following applies to these imaginary surfaces:
1367           1. The heliport primary surface described in 14 CFR Part 77 coincides in size and
1368              shape with the designated FATO. This surface is a horizontal plane at the elevation
1369              of the established heliport elevation.
1370           2. The approach surface (and, by reciprocal, the departure surface) begins at the edge
1371              of the heliport primary surface with the same width as the primary surface and
1372              extends outward and upward for a horizontal distance of 4,000 feet (1,219 m) where
1373              its width is 500 feet (152 m). The slope of the approach surface is 8:1, horizontal
1374              units and vertical units respectively.
1375           3. The transitional surfaces extend outward and upward from the lateral boundaries of
1376              the primary surface and from the approach surfaces at a slope of 2:1, horizontal
1377              units and vertical units respectively, for 250 feet (76 m) measured horizontally from
1378              the centerline of the primary and approach surfaces. The transitional surfaces extend
1379              longitudinally from the edge of the FATO to the far end of the approach/departure
1380              surface. The transitional surface does not apply to the FATO edge opposite the
1381              approach/departure surface.
1382           Ensure the approach, transitional, and primary surfaces are clear of penetrations (except
1383           for navigational aids that are fixed-by-function) unless an FAA aeronautical study
1384           determines any penetrations to these surfaces are not hazards. See 14 CFR Part 77.9(d)
1385           to determine when notification is required and when aeronautical studies are conducted.




                                                       4-23
       10/22/2026                                 DRAFT                                   AC 150/5390-4


1386   4.7.3   Obstructions.
1387           All imaginary surfaces must be clear of obstructions unless an FAA aeronautical study
1388           determines otherwise.
1389           The FAA conducts such aeronautical studies at:
1390           1. A public use airport listed in the Airport/Facility Directory, Alaska Supplement, or
1391              Pacific Chart Supplement of the U.S. Government Flight Information Publications.
1392           2. A military airport under construction, or an airport under construction that will be
1393              available for public use.
1394           3. An airport operated by a Federal agency or the Department of Defense (DoD).
1395           4. An airport or heliport with at least one FAA-approved instrument approach
1396              procedure.
1397           Traverse ways are considered obstructions. The mobile objects that go over roads,
1398           railroads, waterways, or other traverse ways, must be accounted for within the limits of
1399           the approach, departure, and transitional surfaces. For evaluating traverse ways, use the
1400           following guidelines:
1401           1. Consider the height of the highest mobile object that normally traverses the area
1402              under the approach surface.
1403           2. If unknown, apply the following typical values above the traverse way surface:
1404                a. interstate highways: 17 feet (5.2 m)
1405                b. railroads: 23 feet (7 m)
1406           3. access controlled roads: 10 feet (3 m)
1407           4. all other public roads, highways, and vehicle parking areas: 15 feet (4.6 m).
1408           If approach and departure paths are provided that are shallower than an 8:1, clear the
1409           path of obstructions. Regularly re-examine obstacles near approach/departure paths to
1410           prevent the encroachment of hazards. Include an appraisal of tree growth near approach
1411           and departure paths in this re-examination.

1704   4.9.8   Visual Glideslope Indicators (VGSI).
1705           A VGSI is equipment that provides pilots with visual vertical course and descent cues
1706           (Figure 4-25). A VGSI can be in the form of a Precision Approach Path Indicators
1707           (PAPI), Visual Approach Slope Indicator (VASI), Tricolor VASI, Pulsating VASI, and
1708           Helicopter Approach Path Indicator (HAPI) systems. Install a VGSI when one or more
1709           of the following conditions exist, especially at night:
1710           1. Site specific obstacle clearance conditions require a unique slope to be flown.
1711           2. Noise abatement or traffic control procedures necessitate a specific slope to be
1712              flown.
1713           3. The environment of the heliport provides few visual surface cues.

1714           4.9.8.1      Siting and Installation.
1715                        1. The optimum location of a VGSI is on the extended centerline of the
1716                           approach path at a distance that brings the aircraft to a hover with the
1717                           undercarriage between 3 and 8 feet (0.9 to 2.4 m) above the TLOF.
1718                        2. To properly locate the VGSI, estimate the vertical distance from the
1719                           undercarriage to the pilot’s eye.
1720                        3. Install the VGSI so the lowest on-course visual signal provides a
1721                           minimum of one degree of clearance over any object that lies within
1722                           ten degrees of the approach course centerline.
1723                        4. The VGSI may be pilot controlled so that is it only “on” when needed.

1764   4.12     Fall Protection and Means of Egress.
1765            If the heliport is elevated, consult Title 29 CFR Part 1926.34, Means of Egress, for
1766            access to the surface. Title 29 CFR Part 1926.34 requires two separate access points for
1767            an elevated structure such as an elevated TLOF or FATO.

1768   4.12.1   Standards.
1769            1. Design stairs in compliance with 29 CFR Part 1910.25, Stairways.
1770            2. Design handrails described in this regulation to fold down or be removable to below
1771               the level of the TLOF or FATO so they will not be hazards during aircraft
1772               operations.
1773            3. For hospital heliports, provide access to and from the TLOF surface via a ramp to
1774               provide for quick and easy transportation of a patient on a gurney.
1775                a. Build ramps in accordance with state and local requirements.
1776                b. Design the width of the ramp, and any turns in the ramp, to be wide enough to
1777                   accommodate a gurney with a person walking on each side.
1778                c. Design straight segments of the ramp at least 6 feet (1.8 m) wide.
1779                d. Add additional width in segments of the ramp where it turns.
1780                e. Provide the ramp with a slip-resistant surface.
1781            4. Design elevated heliports to include fall protection around areas where people will
1782               have access (e.g., at edge of TLOF or FATO).
1783                a. Title 29 CFR § 1910.28, Duty to Have Fall Protection and Falling Object
1784                   Protection, requires that the provision of fall protection if the platform is
1785                   elevated 4 feet (1.2 m) or more above its surroundings.
1786                b. Do not use permanent railings or fences that would be safety hazards, such as
1787                   those obstructing or penetrating an imaginary surface, during aircraft operations.
1788            5. Ensure fall protection devices do not penetrate the approach/departure surface or
1789               transitional surface.

1790   4.12.2   Recommended Practices.
1791            1. Add fall protection for all platforms elevated 30 inches (0.8 m) or more.
1792            2. Safety nets are a potential solution to protect against falls and falling objects.


                                                       4-44
       10/22/2026                                   DRAFT                                   AC 150/5390-4


1793                a. Ensure safety nets meet state and local regulations.
1794                b. Install safety nets at least 5 feet (1.5 m) wide.
1795                c. Fasten the insides and outside edges of the nets to a solid structure.
1796                d. Ensure the net is made with materials that are resistant to environmental effects.
1797                e. Inspect the net annually for integrity.
1798                f. Ensure the net has a load carrying capability of 25 lbs per square foot (122 kg/sq
1799                   m).
1800                g. For heliports with non-loadbearing FATOs, attach the net to the outer TLOF
1801                   perimeter frame at or below the edge elevation of the TLOF.


1802   4.13     Electromagnetic Effects.
1803            Aircraft are susceptible to electromagnetic effects. Nearby electromagnetic devices,
1804            such as a large ventilator motor, elevator motor, magnetic resonance imaging machine
1805            (MRI), or other devices that consume large amounts of electricity may cause temporary
1806            abnormalities in the aircraft magnetic compass and interfere with other onboard
1807            navigational equipment. Buried rebar or other objects made of iron/steel below the
1808            heliport surface have also been shown to interfere with an aircraft’s navigation
1809            instruments.

1835                                  CHAPTER 5. Heliport Taxiways


1836   5.1     Taxiways.
1837           Taxiways provide for the movement of aircraft from one part of the heliport to another
1838           via either ground taxi or hover taxi operation. They connect the TLOF and FATO to the
1839           parking positions and allow for maneuvering when needed. When the TLOF is separate
1840           from the FATO, taxiways connect the TLOF and the FATO via hover taxi operations.
1841           The taxiway does not require a prepared surface if it is designed solely for hover
1842           operations. Taxiways are not needed at heliports with no parking spots, refueling, or
1843           recharging areas outside a TLOF unless the TLOF is separate from a FATO.
1844           For a taxiway with a prepared ground surface, the taxiway consists of two components:
1845           1. Taxiway surface – The width of the pavement or other prepared surface where the
1846              aircraft wheels touch.
1847           2. TOFA – The area free of objects either side of the taxiway centerline.

1848   5.1.1   Standards.
1849           1. Design taxiways to the dimensions in Table 5-1.
1850           2. No objects are permitted in the taxiway or TOFA except those fixed-by-function.
1851           3. Site fixed-by-function objects, like taxiway lights, at the edge of the minimum
1852              taxiway surface width and on frangible mounts when elevated.
1853           4. Design the hover taxiway to consist of only the TOFA.
1854           5. Design hover taxiways so that aircraft do not fly over other operating areas (e.g.,
1855              parking areas, taxiways, TLOF, FATO, etc.).
1856           6. For ground taxiways, provide a Portland cement concrete, asphalt, or stabilized
1857              surface, such as turf, under the standards of AC 150/5370-10.
1858           7. For unpaved portions of taxiways, provide a turf cover or treat the surface in a way
1859              to prevent dirt and debris from being dispersed by a taxiing aircraft’s DWOW.
1860           8. Design taxiway longitudinal gradients to not exceed 2 percent.
1861           9. Design transverse gradients to be between 0.5 percent and 2 percent.

1966                                   CHAPTER 6. Heliport Parking


1967   6.1     Parking.
1968           The design of a parking position is based on the size and weight of the aircraft intended
1969           to use it.

1970   6.1.1   Standards.

1971           6.1.1.1      Location.
1972                        Do not locate aircraft parking areas underneath approach/departure
1973                        surfaces.

1974           6.1.1.2      Size.
1975                        1. Design the individual parking positions to accommodate the aircraft
1976                           sizes and weights expected to use the positions, and size at least one
1977                           parking position to accommodate the critical aircraft.
1978                        2. Design parking to 1 D.
1979                        3. For parking positions that will allow hover and/or air taxi operations,
1980                           design the parking position no smaller than the FATO size and meet
1981                           dynamic FATO load-bearing requirements.
1982                        4. Account for the additional space needed if turning the aircraft while in
1983                           the parking position.
1984                        5. Design parking to have rotors and propellers away from the direction
1985                           of passenger walking paths.

1986           6.1.1.3      Shape.
1987                        Design parking positions to be round, rectangular, or polygons shaped and
1988                        designed as taxi-through, turnaround, or back-out (Figure 6-1 and Figure
1989                        6-2).

1990           6.1.1.4      Separation.
1991                        1. Provide a minimum of 0.28 D, no less than 10 feet (3 m) separation
1992                           between the parking position and any object, building, safety area, or
1993                           other parking position.
1994                        2. Increase the minimum separation from 0.28 D to 0.42 D for back out
1995                           parking.
1996                        3. Use the largest aircraft expected to occupy a parking position to
1997                           determine the separation between parking positions intended for
1998                           aircraft of different sizes.
1999                        4. Use the critical aircraft to determine the separation between parking
2000                           positions and taxiways.

2084                                    CHAPTER 7. Soil Stabilization


2085           Stabilize soil to meet different site requirements. Find guidance on soil stabilization in
2086           AC 150/5320-6 and AC 150/5370-10, at the FAA Airports website
2087           https://www.faa.gov/airports.


2088   7.1     Design Considerations.
2089           1. Consider aircraft weight, ground support vehicle weight, operational frequency, soil
2090              analysis, and climatic conditions in selecting the method(s) and extent of surface
2091              stabilization. A well-drained and well-established turf that presents a smooth, dense
2092              surface is usually the most cost-effective surface stabilization available.
2093           2. Thicker pavements may be needed where the quality of the subsurface soil is poor
2094              or questionable.
2095           3. Climatic and soil conditions dictate the appropriate grass species to use at the site.
2096           4. Where heliports are located on soils that have poor load-carrying capabilities when
2097              wet, consider overcoming this deficiency by mixing selected granular materials into
2098              the upper 12 inches (0.3 m) of the soil (see Item P-217 of AC 150/5370-10).
2099                a. Suitable granular materials for this purpose are crushed stone, pit-run gravel,
2100                   coarse sand, or oyster shells.
2101                b. Use a sufficient ratio of aggregate to soil to improve the stability of the soil yet
2102                   retain the soil’s ability to support grass.
2103           5. Precast masonry shapes or metal panels/grates may be used to support soil and
2104              loads.
2105                a. Use them on a prepared foundation to present a semi-solid surface that allows
2106                   grass to grow in the natural openings.
2107                b. Other materials such as pre-manufactured concrete or other suitable materials
2108                   may be used where applicable.
2109           6. Ensure any materials used do not become a flying hazard due to DWOW.

2110                             CHAPTER 8. Aircraft Fueling and Charging


2111   8.1     Fueling.
2112           Various federal, state, and local requirements for petroleum handling facilities apply to
2113           systems for storing and dispensing fuel. Guidance is provided in AC 150/5230-4,
2114           Aircraft Fuel Storage, Handling, and Dispensing on Airports.

2115   8.1.1   Standards.
2116           1. Design the heliport facility to allow fueling with the use of a fuel truck (this
2117              includes hot refueling) or a specific fueling area with stationary fuel tanks.
2118           2. Do not locate fueling equipment in the TLOF or FATO.
2119           3. Do not locate fueling equipment directly under the approach/departure surfaces.
2120           4. Design and mark fueling facilities to minimize the potential for aircraft to collide
2121              with the dispensing equipment.
2122           5. Design fueling areas so there is no object tall enough to be hit by any part of the
2123              critical aircraft.
2124                a. Provide a minimum of 0.42 D clearance from any part of the aircraft and fueling
2125                   equipment.
2126                b. Provide area lighting for the fueling area if night fueling operations are offered.
2127                c. Ensure any light poles do not constitute an obstruction hazard.

2128   8.1.2   Design Consideration.
2129           Low-profile fuel dispensing cabinets can be in one quadrant of a safety area if there is
2130           no available room outside the safety area, provided they are marked and lighted, and the
2131           other three quadrants of the safety area are clear.


2132   8.2     Charging and Electric Infrastructure.
2133           Many emerging vertical flight aircraft are being developed to use electric propulsion.
2134           The electrical needs for these aircraft vary based on design and manufacturer. This AC
2135           addresses battery driven technologies. Future guidance will be provided on other
2136           emerging energy concepts (e.g., hydrogen).
2137           Electrification of aviation propulsion systems is an evolving area with few industry-
2138           specific standards. In addition to relevant national, state, and local building codes,
2139           Appendix J provides a partial list of relevant standards that may assist when specifying
2140           charging systems and facility layout for this emerging industry.
2141           At the time of this publication, consensus has not been achieved regarding classes of
2142           charging or connection standards and could vary based on the aircraft duty cycle,
2143           charging speed, battery chemistry, charging system, and battery cooling system, etc.




                                                       8-1
       10/22/2026                                 DRAFT                                    AC 150/5390-4


2144   8.2.1   Standards.
2145           1. Site aircraft batteries storage outside the TLOF, FATO, and safety areas and not
2146              penetrating the approach/departure surfaces.
2147           2. Ensure charging equipment meets all applicable building codes, fire codes, and
2148              NFPA Standards.

2149   8.2.2   Recommended Practices.
2150           Electric charging infrastructure often exists within the airport environment where other
2151           types of legacy based fueling and aircraft operations exist. Plan this mixed-use
2152           environment to ensure a safe, efficient, and successful airport operation. The following
2153           non-exhaustive list may help with this goal:
2154           1. Ensure the location of charging equipment does not flood. Install equipment on
2155              raised concrete pad to limit exposure.
2156           2. Install collision barriers and signage to limit the potential for impact to equipment
2157              by aircraft, fuel trucks, etc.
2158           3. Provide for proper set back of equipment based on aircraft wingspans, rotor spans,
2159              and propulsion unit spans. Ensure there is no object tall enough to be hit by any part
2160              of the critical aircraft. Provide a minimum of 0.42 D clearance from any part of the
2161              aircraft.
2162           4. Take precautions to ensure snow does not accumulate on and around equipment.
2163              Locate charging equipment so it is clearly visible to snow removal equipment
2164              operators.
2165           5. Do not locate charging equipment in areas where deicing will take place as deicers
2166              contain chemicals that can damage and corrode it.
2167           6. Place charging equipment inside the security fence to limit potential for tampering.
2168           7. Provide for cord storage on the dispensers or use cord retractors to increase safety
2169              and reduce tripping hazards
2170           8. Consider adding options for ground power unit auxiliary needs.
2171           9. Ensure the charging station is located outside the safety area and does not penetrate

2174           1. Consider adapting charging infrastructure design for heliports to multiple aircraft
2175              specific systems.

2179   9.1     Fire Fighting Considerations.
2180           Heliports are subject to state and local rescue and fire fighting regulations. See NFPA
2181           418 and NFPA 855 for the most current guidance on fire protection.

2182   9.1.1   Standards.
2183           1. Provide a fire hose cabinet or extinguisher at each access gate/door and each fueling
2184              location.
2185           2. Locate fire hose cabinets, fire extinguishers, and other fire fighting equipment
2186              adjacent to, but below the level of, the TLOF.


2187   9.2     Security and Safety.
2188           Controlling heliport access and keeping operational areas clear of people, animals,
2189           equipment, debris, and vehicles is important for safety and security.

2190   9.2.1   Standards.
2191           1. For ground-level heliports, erect a safety barrier fence around the aircraft
2192              operational areas outside of the safety area and below the applicable elevation of the
2193              approach/departure and transitional surfaces.
2194           2. Design safety fences to be high enough to present a positive deterrent to people
2195              inadvertently or maliciously entering an operational area, but at a low enough
2196              elevation to be non-hazardous to all aircraft operations.
2197           3. Control access to airside areas with adequate security measures as required or
2198              recommended by the Transportation Security Administration.
2199           4. Locate a heliport caution sign at all heliport access points, ensuring it is outside the
2200              safety area and does not penetrate the approach/departure surface or transitional
2201              surface (Figure 9-1).
2202           5. Ensure all people are outside of the DCA when aircraft are operating, as described
2203              in paragraph 4.6.

2204   9.2.2   Design Considerations.
2205           Other types of safety barriers may be used if they provide adequate positive deterrent to
2206           people inadvertently entering an operational area, do not create turbulence during
2207           aircraft operations, and do not attract wildlife or negatively impact safety following the
2208           same precautions for fences.

2254                          APPENDIX B. Instrument Flight Procedures (IFP)


2255            This appendix provides guidance for instrument operations on heliports. Title 14 CFR
2256            Part 97, Standard Instrument Procedures, prescribes the criteria for instrument flight
2257            procedure development through incorporation of FAA Order 8260.3, United States
2258            Standard for Terminal Instrument Procedures (TERPS). Instrument flight procedures
2259            permit aircraft operations to continue during periods of low ceilings and reduced
2260            visibility. Instrument flight procedures include approach procedures, departure
2261            procedures, and missed approach procedures.
2262            The FAA establishes instrument flight procedures under FAA 8260-series Orders
2263            overseen by the FAA Flight Technologies and Procedures Division. When a procedure
2264            applies to a private (non-public) heliport, is developed for one specific user, or is
2265            developed by a non-FAA service provider using unique FAA-approved instrument
2266            criteria, the instrument procedure is a “special” instrument procedure. After approval by
2267            the FAA, the special instrument procedure is issued to the operator approved to fly the
2268            procedure.
2269            Those who design instrument flight procedures have some flexibility in the design of
2270            such procedures. See the latest revision of FAA 8260-series Orders for more detailed
2271            information on criteria for developing instrument flight procedures. The standards and
2272            recommendations in this AC are not intended to be sufficient to design an instrument
2273            flight procedure. Initiate early contact with the Flight Technologies and Procedures
2274            Division | Federal Aviation Administration to plan for and establish instrument flight
2275            procedures.

2344                APPENDIX C. Emergency Helicopter /VTOL Landing Facilities (EHLF)


2345   C.1     General.
2346           Preplanning emergency landing areas will result in safer and more effective air-support
2347           operations. These facilities comprise rooftop emergency facilities and medical
2348           emergency sites and are not for routine aircraft operations. For additional reference
2349           material, see paragraph 1.5.


2350   C.2     Notification and Coordination.
2351           In addition to the sponsor’s requirements to provide notice under 14 CFR Part 157,
2352           advise the local Terminal Approach Radar Control or the local ATC facility manager in
2353           writing of the EHLF. State and local building code requirements may apply to rooftop
2354           EHLF facilities.


2355   C.3     Rooftop Emergency Facilities.
2356           Review local building codes to determine if they require structures over a specified
2357           height to provide a clear area on the roof capable of accommodating an aircraft to
2358           facilitate firefighting or emergency evacuation operations.

2359   C.3.1   Standards.

2360           C.3.1.1       TLOF.
2361                         1. Design the landing surface to the local fire department requirements
2362                            based on the size and weight of the aircraft expected to engage in fire
2363                            or rescue operations (Figure C-1).
2364                         2. Design the TLOF to be square, rectangular, or circular in configuration
2365                            and centered within the EHLF.
2366                         3. Design the length and width or diameter to be at least the controlling
2367                            dimension D of the largest aircraft expected to use the EHLF.
2368                         4. Design the TLOF to accommodate the maximum takeoff weight of the
2369                            critical aircraft expected to use the EHLF.
2370                         5. Provide two pedestrian access points to the TLOF at least 90 degrees
2371                            apart with a minimum of 60 feet (18.3 m) TLOF perimeter separation.
2372                         6. Design the surface so drainage flows away from pedestrian access
2373                            points, with a maximum slope of 1.5 to 2.0 percent.

2483                APPENDIX G. Design Standards for Heliport Perimeter Lighting


2484   G.1     Elevated and In-pavement Omnidirectional Helipad Perimeter Light.
2485           Approaches to heliports are typically performed into the prevailing wind direction.
2486           Aircraft can approach the heliport from any direction. The approach paths flown by the
2487           pilot dictate the required vertical intensity coverage of the light fixture.

2488   G.1.1   Standards.

2489           G.1.1.1      Horizontal Beam Coverage.
2490                        Ensure the intensity for heliport perimeter lighting is maintained for all
2491                        possible directions of approach, requiring an omnidirectional horizontal
2492                        light geometry.

2493           G.1.1.2      Vertical Beam Coverage.
2494                        1. Control the vertical intensity distribution of the heliport perimeter
2495                           lights as a function of elevation (Table G-1).
2496                            a. The approach path data were converted to the corresponding angle
2497                               of elevation from the heliport perimeter lighting as a function of
2498                               range.
2499                            b. The mean angle of elevation and the upper and lower limits of the
2500                               vertical beam spread will increase as the range from the heliport
2501                               decreases.


2502                               Table G-1. Approach Angles Assuming VMC

                                   Approach                 Typical Descent Angle
                              Shallow                        3 degrees to 5 degrees
                              Normal                        7 degrees to 12 degrees
                              Steep                         12 degrees to 15 degrees

2505                        used by industry to produce and install optimized perimeter lighting. The
2506                        intensity specification is based on a currently available light fixture FAA
2507                        type L-860E that was determined to be the most effective in meeting the
2508                        need for the pilot to be able to clearly see the perimeter lighting.

2538           for each 1 degree vertically over 360 degrees horizontally (5-degree intervals). For
2539           vertical angles of 10 to 90 degrees, visually verify the projected light intensity.

2546           All subsequent L-860H and L-852H light fixture production will be based upon the

2561                          APPENDIX I. Short Takeoff and Landing (STOL)


2562           As short takeoff and landing (STOL) aircraft are developed with new technologies such
2563           as distributed electrical propulsion (DEP) and blown lift, there may be the desire and
2564           ability for these aircraft to utilize heliport facilities. Blown lift using DEP accelerates air
2565           over the wing, which generates lift at a much slower forward air speed than
2566           conventional airplanes. This makes a heliport a potential takeoff and landing area for
2567           these types of aircraft. In these instances, elongated load-bearing TLOFs and an
2568           elongated FATO would likely be standard to account for the distance needed to take off
2569           and land. Other standards may be needed to account for the stopping distance required
2570           in all weather conditions, including load-bearing safety areas and other surfaces
2571           typically associated with runways. Additionally, consider potential prop wash or jet
2572           blast. The FAA will release more guidance for these novel distributed propulsion blown
2573           lift systems as data becomes available. If a facility sponsor expects a STOL aircraft to
2574           utilize their heliport facility, coordinate with the applicable FAA ADO and FSDO
2575           office.
